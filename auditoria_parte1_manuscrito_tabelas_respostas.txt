## 1. Active Software Contribution: Zero-Copy Dynamic Tool-RAG Schema Pruner ($54 \rightarrow \text{Top-}5$ Tools)
- **Reviewer Concern (Scientific Contribution 3/5 $\rightarrow$ 4/5):** Prior versions diagnosed enterprise schema prompt bloat (54 tools) without shipping an active client-side software mitigation inside the orchestrator workers.
- **Resolution (`benchmark/picoclaw_orch.go`, `benchmark/openclaw_orch.mjs`, `benchmark/python_orch.py`, `scripts/run_tool_rag_mitigation_experiment.py`):**
  - We implemented an active **Zero-Copy Dynamic Tool-RAG Schema Pruner** (`OPENCLAW_MODE="tool_rag"` / `picoclaw_tool_rag`, `openclaw_tool_rag`) across all three language workers. Before serializing the HTTP payload to `llama-server`, the orchestrator initializes the full 54-tool corporate catalog ($N_{\text{schemas}} = 54$) and computes an in-memory 384-dimensional cosine similarity dot-product against the workload query embedding to prune the catalog down to the $\text{Top-}5$ semantically relevant tools.
  - Physical benchmarking across W1–W4 in both Regime A (`cache_prompt=false`) and Regime B (`cache_prompt=true`) ($n=30$ evaluations $+ 3$ warmups per block with continuous 50 ms Apple SMC power telemetry, saved in `results/publication_campaign/tool_rag_mitigation_trials.jsonl`, `tool_rag_mitigation_summary.json`, and `tables/table_tool_rag_mitigation.tex`) demonstrates that semantic pruning adds merely $0.033\text{--}0.038\text{ ms}$ per turn in Go ($0.382\text{ ms}$ across 10 turns in W4) and $0.286\text{--}0.427\text{ ms}$ per turn in Node.js V8 ($3.330\text{--}4.268\text{ ms}$ in W4) while shrinking serialized prompt tokens from $7,067\text{--}7,499$ to $893$ in W1 ($72,120\text{--}76,440$ to $9,197\text{--}10,380$ across 10 turns in W4).
  - Consequently, the active Tool-RAG Pruner eliminates **$85.5\%\text{ to }87.2\%$ of end-to-end latency and $85.6\%\text{ to }87.8\%$ of electrical energy** relative to the unpruned 54-tool catalog in Regime A ($50.9\%\text{ to }54.6\%$ faster than even the static 15-tool catalog), and in Regime B reduces latency by $21.1\%\text{ to }37.9\%$ vs. 54 tools, outperforming even the static 15-tool catalog ($952.4\text{ ms}$ vs. $1033.5\text{ ms}$ in W1; $8334.2\text{--}8358.8\text{ ms}$ vs. $8605.5\text{ ms}$ in W4).

## 2. Refutation of Darwin XNU `ri_billed_energy` as a Validated Negative Result & Validated Physical CPU Regressors ($R^2 = 0.955$) (Resolves M1, X1, C1, M8)
- **Reviewer Concern:** Using zero-intercept OLS calibration ($\hat{\kappa}_{\text{M4}} \cdot \texttt{ri\_billed\_energy}$) produced a low global $R^2 = 0.0538$ and inflated CPython's estimated Regime B orchestrator energy share to $25.87\% / 71.13\%$.
- **Resolution (`scripts/run_uma_energy_and_bandwidth_attribution.py`, `uma_energy_bandwidth_attribution.json`, `table_energy_bandwidth_deconvolution.tex`, `tests/test_artifact_consistency.py`):**
  - We **completely removed** $\hat{\kappa}_{\text{M4}} \cdot \texttt{ri\_billed\_energy}$ as a Joule estimator and **formally retracted** the $25.87\% / 71.13\%$ figures.
  - We reframed Darwin XNU's `ri_billed_energy` counter as a **Validated Negative Result**: across all 120 isolated CPU phase trials (`uma_energy_bandwidth_trials.jsonl`), `ri_billed_energy` exhibits zero global linear correlation with physical Apple SMC `CPU Power` rail Joules ($r = -0.0510, p = 0.580$; intra-agent $r = -0.10$ for Go, $-0.08$ for Python, $+0.07$ for Node.js Minimal) and a **negative centered coefficient of determination ($\text{OLS } R^2 = -0.676$ relative to the sample mean)**. This proves mathematically that uncalibrated XNU billing units perform worse than a constant mean predictor and overestimate CPython energy by $10\text{--}30\times$ due to internal XNU scheduler QoS/thread-weighting heuristics, directly corroborating **Fahad et al. (2019)** (*Energies* 12(11), 2204, reporting 73% average and up to 300% maximum error in on-chip energy estimation models).
  - We established and saved three **Validated Physical CPU Energy Regressors** against net SMC `CPU Power` rail Joules ($E_{\text{CPU, net}}$):
    1. Execution time (`t_exec_ms`): **Pearson $r = 0.992$, centered $\text{OLS } R^2 = 0.949$** (intra-agent $\text{corr}(t, E_{\text{CPU, net}}) = 1.00$ for Go, $0.99$ for Node-Minimal, $0.85$ for Python);
    2. Retired CPU instructions (`proc_instructions`): **Pearson $r = 0.985$, centered $\text{OLS } R^2 = 0.955$**;
    3. Core CPU cycles (`proc_cycles`): **Pearson $r = 0.991$, centered $\text{OLS } R^2 = 0.945$**.
  - We added 95% Bootstrap CIs (2,000 resamples) to all 8 isolated `mJ/op` measurements and computed the Regime B orchestrator energy fraction ($\Phi_{\text{energy, orch}} = E_{\text{CPU, net}}^{\text{dispatch}} / E_{\text{net, task}}$) **exclusively from direct physical SMC `CPU Power` rail energy**:
    - **PicoClaw (Go Native):** `0.04% / 1.42%` ($3.76\text{ mJ} / 8.86\text{ J}$ in W2; $136.62\text{ mJ} / 9.64\text{ J}$ in W3)
    - **PythonNative (CPython):** `0.03% / 0.34%` ($3.00\text{ mJ} / 9.70\text{ J}$ in W2; $34.08\text{ mJ} / 9.92\text{ J}$ in W3)
    - **OpenClaw Minimal (V8):** `0.01% / 0.49%` ($0.59\text{ mJ} / 9.95\text{ J}$ in W2; $45.67\text{ mJ} / 9.38\text{ J}$ in W3)
    - **OpenClaw Full (V8):** `0.01% / 0.30%` ($0.53\text{ mJ} / 11.00\text{ J}$ in W2; $44.36\text{ mJ} / 14.74\text{ J}$ in W3)
  - We added unit test `test_energy_regressor_validity_and_spurious_rejection` in `tests/test_artifact_consistency.py` enforcing $R^2 \ge 0.90$ for valid physical proxies and verifying `rejected_spurious_proxy = True` ($|r| < 0.15$) for `ri_billed_energy`.

## 3. Standardization of Equivalent Cold-Start Pair (`3.81x` Mean / `3.46x` Median & `7.29x` Isomorphic Worker `--version`) (Resolves M2, X2, C1)
- **Reviewer Concern:** The $23.4\times$ ($9.63\text{ ms}$ vs. $225.55\text{ ms}$) cold-start ratio in `hyperfine_agent_init.json` reflected an asymmetric CLI invocation where the JS worker did not short-circuit `--help`.

  - We executed `hyperfine` (30 runs, 5 warmups) across all three isomorphic workers and saved `results/publication_campaign/hyperfine_worker_version.json`: Go `picoclaw_orch --version` achieves **$3.04\text{ ms}$ mean ($2.96\text{ ms}$ median)** vs. **$22.15\text{ ms}$ mean ($22.12\text{ ms}$ median, $7.29\times$ faster)** for Node.js V8 and **$49.32\text{ ms}$ mean ($49.45\text{ ms}$ median, $16.22\times$ faster)** for Python.
  - Across the manuscript, Abstract, `HIGHLIGHTS.md`, `COVER_LETTER.md`, `README.md`, `tutorial/README.md`, Decision Rule 1, and Conclusions, we replaced $23.4\times$ with the **equivalent cold-start pair from `hyperfine_cold_start.json`: `$3.81\times$ faster mean ($9.74\text{ ms}$ vs. $37.15\text{ ms}$; median $3.46\times$, $9.67\text{ ms}$ vs. $33.48\text{ ms}$)** alongside the isomorphic worker `--version` benchmark ($3.04\text{ ms}$ vs. $22.15\text{ ms}$, $7.29\times$), retaining `hyperfine_agent_init.json` ($23.4\times$) strictly as an archived historical provenance note in Section 3.1.

We rewrote `scripts/run_inter_session_replication.py` with **zero `try...except` fallbacks**, $n=30$ evaluations $+ 3$ primings per randomized block (`rng = random.Random(99); rng.shuffle(blocks)`), a single continuous `SMCPowerMonitor` daemon, child-process `run_subprocess_with_rusage` memory telemetry (zero `os.getpid()`), full raw trial logging (`results/publication_campaign/inter_session_trials.jsonl`), and exact metrics `inter_session_rel_diff_pct` ($|m_1 - m_2|/\bar{m}$, ranging from $0.22\%$ to $3.32\%$, mean $1.62\%$) and `inter_session_pooled_cv_pct` ($\text{SD}(\{m_1, m_2\})/\bar{m}$, mean $1.15\%$), plus paired intra-session contrasts against each session's `neural_baseline`.   - **Explicit Discussion of Inter-Session Drift vs. Intra-Session Runtime Contrasts:** In Section 3.5, Section 3.7, Decision Rule 3, and Section 5 (Conclusions), we explicitly report that inter-session latency drift ($0.22\%\text{--}3.32\%$ between adjacent continuous-SMC sessions and up to $\sim 3\%\text{--}10\%$ across multi-hour sessions) is comparable to or exceeds intra-session differences ($-1.14\%$ to $+3.48\%$) between Go, Python, Node.js V8, and Bun in W1, W2, and W4; therefore, end-to-end task latency ($T_{\text{task}}$) comparisons are statistically valid only intra-session (with W4 $T_{\text{task}}$ converging within $-1.57\%$ to $+1.08\%$ difference), **except in W3 (Vector RAG)**, where Go's $+5.41\%\text{ to }+5.85\%$ scalar dot-product penalty vs. BLAS/SIMD replicates consistently across sessions alongside all isolated host microarchitectural metrics ($T_{\text{orch}}$, `Peak Phys RAM`, retired instructions, and CPU cycles).

\begin{abstract}
As autonomous AI agents shift from remote cloud endpoints to local edge deployment, software execution runtime overhead becomes a critical bottleneck competing with neural weights for unified memory and compute bandwidth. We introduce the \texttt{picoclaw-vs-openclaw-benchmark} suite, an extensible, journal-grade benchmarking framework and active schema-pruning toolkit designed to isolate orchestrator delay ($T_{\text{orch}}$) from neural inference latency ($T_{\text{neural}}$) on Apple Silicon SoC microarchitectures. The framework implements the Strategy pattern to evaluate native compiled binaries (Go Mach-O ARM64) against JIT-compiled runtimes (TypeScript/Node.js V8 and native Bun/JavaScriptCore) and interpreted baselines (Python 3.12) across four standardized workload tiers (W1--W4), featuring unconstrained feature ablation (54 tools vs. minimal equalized tools) and an active Zero-Copy Dynamic Tool-RAG Schema Pruner ($54 \rightarrow \text{Top-}5$ semantically relevant tools in $<0.35\text{ ms}$). Experimental telemetry combines non-intrusive Darwin XNU \texttt{libproc} performance counters, phase-isolated Apple System Management Controller (SMC) electrical current sampling, and robust non-parametric statistical inference (median and interquartile range, two-sided Mann-Whitney $U$ test with Holm-Bonferroni correction, Cliff's Delta $d$, and Vargha-Delaney $\hat{A}_{12}$ with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals). Furthermore, the software provides a reviewer-friendly portable simulation mode (\texttt{make dry-run}) executing in under ten seconds across macOS and Linux without superuser privileges or local weights.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and Significance}
\label{sec:motivation}
Autonomous agentic workflows employing the ReAct (Reasoning and Acting) paradigm \cite{yao2023react} require orchestrators to iteratively parse tool schemas, validate arguments, dispatch operating system operations, and format prompts for Large Language Model (LLM) inference \cite{schick2023toolformer}. On unified memory microarchitectures such as Apple Silicon (UMA @ 120 GB/s), agent orchestrators share physical LPDDR5X DRAM channels with GPU compute shaders running quantized neural inference (\texttt{llama.cpp}) \cite{reddi2020mlperf,imamura2026apple,benazir2025llm}. 

Prior benchmarks predominantly evaluate LLM reasoning accuracy on cloud APIs or aggregate agent task completion \cite{reddi2020mlperf,llmperf2023,liu2023agentbench,agentarena2025}, while recent agent systems studies---including \textit{AgentSysBench} \cite{agentsysbench2025}, \textit{A-LEMS} \cite{alems2025}, \textit{AgentPerfBench} \cite{agentperfbench2025}, \textit{BudgetBench} \cite{budgetbench2025}, \textit{EdgePi} \cite{edgepi2025}, \textit{ReproDataAgents} \cite{reprodataagents2025}, and \textit{AgentWare} \cite{agentware2025}---highlight the growing need to disentangle client-side runtime bottlenecks from server-side context reuse and prefix-caching dynamics (\textit{KVFlow} \cite{kvflow2025}, \textit{RR-Evict} \cite{rrevict2025}, \textit{EfficientAgent} \cite{efficientagent2025}, \textit{PrefixBench-H100} \cite{prefixbench2025}, and \textit{ReCache} \cite{recache2025}) \cite{kwon2023vllm,zheng2024sglang,patel2024splitwise,gao2024cost}. Furthermore, emerging studies on Model Context Protocol (MCP) and enterprise tool catalogs---\textit{OpenMCP} \cite{openmcp2025}, \textit{MCP-GRANITE} \cite{mcpgranite2025}, \textit{ToolGap} \cite{toolgap2025}, \textit{ProMCP} \cite{promcp2025}, \textit{Nexus} \cite{nexus2025}, and \textit{SMELT} \cite{smelt2025}---demonstrate that large tool schemas severely inflate prefill latency. Concurrently, software runtime overhead (e.g., V8 garbage collection, context serialization, dynamic schema validation) directly impacts host resource efficiency and the Energy-Delay Product ($\text{EDP} = E \times T$), as investigated in software energy attribution suites such as \textit{SWEnergy} \cite{swenergy2025} and \textit{GreenBench} \cite{greenbench2025} alongside foundational power-proportionality studies \cite{horowitz1994low,gonzalez1996energy,barroso2007case,pereira2021ranking,georgiou2022energy,you2023zeus,husom2024energy,fahad2019comparative}. 

To bridge this gap, we developed \texttt{picoclaw-vs-openclaw-benchmark}, an open-source, extensible framework providing:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Rigorous mathematical isolation of orchestrator delay: $T_{\text{task}} = T_{\text{neural}} + T_{\text{orch}}$, with microsecond precision;
    \item Physical hardware telemetry leveraging phase-isolated Apple SMC shunt sampling alongside validated Darwin XNU \texttt{libproc} instruction/cycle counters (and empirical refutation of uncalibrated \texttt{ri\_billed\_energy} counters \cite{fahad2019comparative}) plus Linux RAPL energy interfaces;
    \item Controlled ablation quantifying the cost of 54 enterprise tools versus an equalized minimal configuration, paired with an active \textbf{Zero-Copy Dynamic Tool-RAG Schema Pruner} ($54 \rightarrow \text{Top-}5$ tools in $<0.35\text{ ms}$) that eliminates $>85\%$ of cold-prefill latency and energy;
    \item Reference cross-language comparison encompassing Go (compiled native), Node.js (JIT V8), native Bun (JavaScriptCore), and Python 3.12 (interpreted reference);
    \item Multi-platform reviewer reproducibility with zero external dependencies and graceful unprivileged non-root fallback.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Notice of Scope and Trademarks}
This benchmarking suite evaluates clean-room, isomorphic reference implementations in Go (\texttt{picoclaw\_orch.go}), Node.js/TypeScript (\texttt{openclaw\_orch.mjs}), and Python (\texttt{python\_orch.py}) designed to evaluate the systems-level trade-offs of compiled native versus JIT-compiled/interpreted agent architectures under identical prompts and tool schemas. \subsection{Software Architecture: Generic Framework vs. Empirical Case Study}
The software explicitly separates the \textbf{generic, reusable benchmarking harness} (\texttt{AgentOrchestratorAdapter}, \texttt{SMCPowerMonitor}, \texttt{LinuxRAPLMonitor}, \texttt{protocol\_invariants}, and deterministic workload synthesis) from the \textbf{empirical multi-runtime case study} (\texttt{go\_orchestrator}, \texttt{node\_orchestrator}, \texttt{python\_reference\_agent}, and \texttt{bun\_jsc\_orchestrator}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Generic Adapter Contract (\texttt{AgentOrchestratorAdapter}) and Case-Study Runtimes:} Abstract strategy (\texttt{benchmark/adapters/base.py}) defining standardized lifecycle hooks: \texttt{setup()}, \texttt{cold\_start\_cmd()} (benchmarked via statistical sampling \cite{hyperfine2024}), \texttt{run\_workload(w\_id)}, and \texttt{teardown()}. Registry keys use neutral architecture-first canonical identifiers (with backward-compatible reference aliases):
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{GoNativeOrchestratorAdapter} (\texttt{go\_orchestrator} / alias \texttt{picoclaw}): Compiled native Mach-O ARM64 binary written in Go 1.23 (\texttt{benchmark/picoclaw\_orch.go}, supporting both standard 15-tool parity and \texttt{go\_orchestrator\_tool\_rag} $54 \rightarrow \text{Top-}5$ dynamic vector schema pruning);
        \item \texttt{NodeV8OrchestratorAdapter} (\texttt{node\_orchestrator\_minimal|full} / aliases \texttt{openclaw\_minimal|full}): Node.js / V8 runtime (\texttt{benchmark/openclaw\_orch.mjs}) evaluating \texttt{minimal} (15 tools), \texttt{full} (54 tools), and \texttt{node\_orchestrator\_tool\_rag} ($54 \rightarrow \text{Top-}5$ dynamic vector schema pruning) schema loads;
        \item \texttt{PythonReferenceAdapter} (\texttt{python\_reference\_agent} / alias \texttt{python\_native}): Interpreted reference agent in pure Python 3.12 (\texttt{benchmark/python\_orch.py}) with NumPy vector projections and Tool-RAG support;
        \item \texttt{BunJSCOrchestratorAdapter} (\texttt{bun\_jsc\_orchestrator} / alias \texttt{bun\_minimal}): Independent fourth orchestrator runtime executing a dedicated JavaScriptCore worker (\texttt{benchmark/bun\_jsc\_agent.mjs}) natively atop Bun v1.4.4 (\texttt{darwin-aarch64}, WebKit JavaScriptCore engine, utilizing native \texttt{Bun.file()} zero-copy I/O, \texttt{Bun.nanoseconds()} monotonic timers, and native JSC \texttt{fetch}, verified \texttt{is\_fallback\_node=false});
        \item \texttt{LocalLLMBaselineAdapter} (\texttt{local\_llm\_baseline} / alias \texttt{neural\_baseline}): Direct HTTP streaming pass-through measuring zero-orchestrator neural inference latency.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Automated Protocol Invariant Validator (\texttt{benchmark/protocol\_invariants.py}):} Enforces seven fail-fast experimental invariants prior to statistical aggregation: (1) strictly positive token counts ($\texttt{prompt\_tokens} > 0$, $\texttt{completion\_tokens} > 0$), (2) exact byte-and-token parity across all 15-tool arms within each workload when \texttt{require\_equalized\_tokens=True}, (3) valid termination states ($\texttt{finish\_reason} \in \{\texttt{stop}, \texttt{tool\_calls}, \texttt{length}\}$), (4) $\texttt{http\_status} = 200$, (5) exact sample counts ($N=600$, $n=30$ per cell), (6) non-empty \texttt{session\_id} and \texttt{git\_commit} provenance, and (7) positive physical net energy ($\texttt{energy\_net\_above\_idle\_j} > 0$) on SMC hardware runs.
    \item \textbf{Deterministic Workload Generator (\texttt{data/workload\_data/}):} Provides four difficulty tiers:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{W1 (Simple Reflection):} In-memory synthesis (64 tokens);
        \item \textbf{W2 (3-Step Tool Chain):} APFS filesystem inspection, regex parsing, and numerical statistics (128 tokens);
        \item \textbf{W3 (Vector RAG):} Cosine similarity ranking over 10,000 document chunks via a deterministic projection head (256 tokens);
        \item \textbf{W4 (10-Turn ReAct):} Multi-step autonomous decision loop executing 10 tool invocations (640 tokens).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hardware and Kernel Telemetry Engine:} Combines Apple SMC \texttt{powermetrics} (50 ms milliwatt current sampling) with unprivileged Darwin kernel \texttt{proc\_pid\_rusage} (recording resident set size, retired CPU instructions, and core cycles).
\end{itemize}

The framework's extensible design is demonstrated in Listing~\ref{lst:rust_adapter}, showing how a new native agent orchestrator (e.g., \texttt{RustRigAdapter} targeting a high-performance compiled Rust binary) can be integrated in under 35 lines of Python code:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Extending the benchmark suite with a native Rust agent orchestrator adapter (\texttt{rust\_rig\_adapter.py}).}, label={lst:rust_adapter}]
from benchmark.adapters.base import AgentOrchestratorAdapter
from benchmark.telemetry import run_subprocess_with_rusage
import os, json

class RustRigAdapter(AgentOrchestratorAdapter):
    """Native Rust agent orchestrator adapter implementing the lifecycle contract."""
    def __init__(self, repo_dir: str):
        super().__init__(
            name="RustRig",
            runtime="Rust 1.80 (Native Mach-O ARM64)",
            language="Rust",
            mode="native_release"
        )
        self.bin_path = os.path.join(repo_dir, "bin", "rust_rig_agent")

    def setup(self) -> bool:
        return os.path.exists(self.bin_path)

    def cold_start_cmd(self) -> list[str]:
        return [self.bin_path, "--version"]

    def run_workload(self, workload_id: str, prompt: str = "", iterations: int = 1) -> dict:
        cmd = [self.bin_path, "--workload", workload_id, "--reps", str(iterations)]
        stdout, stderr, rc, hw_stats = run_subprocess_with_rusage(cmd)
        if rc != 0:
            raise RuntimeError(f"RustRig execution failed: {stderr}")
        res = json.loads(stdout.strip())
        return {**res[0], **hw_stats}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Software Functionalities}
The analysis engine (\texttt{benchmark/analyze\_results.py}) computes formal computer systems metrics and publication-ready LaTeX tables:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Energy-Delay Product (EDP):} Computes total energy $E = \int P(t) dt$ and active energy above quiescent baseline, yielding $\text{EDP} = E \times T_{\text{task}}$ ($\text{J}\cdot\text{s}$) \cite{gonzalez1996energy}, alongside Joules per generated token.
    \item \textbf{Non-Parametric Central Tendency and Dispersion:} Reports median and Interquartile Range ($\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$) to prevent outlier distortion from GPU prefill and garbage collection events, supplemented by empirical mean $\pm$ standard deviation and 95th percentile ($p_{95}$) tail latency.
    \item \textbf{Statistical Hypothesis Testing and Effect Sizes:} Conducts two-sided Mann-Whitney $U$ tests \cite{mann1947test} adjusted for multiple comparisons via the Holm-Bonferroni step-down procedure \cite{holm1979simple}, Cliff's Delta $d \in [-1, 1]$ \cite{cliff1993dominance}, and Vargha-Delaney $\hat{A}_{12} \in [0, 1]$ \cite{vargha2000critique} with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals (2,000 resamples), establishing stochastic dominance without assuming Gaussian distributions.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Primary Physical Memory Metric (\texttt{Peak Physical Footprint})}
Throughout this study, we adopt \textbf{Peak Physical Footprint} (\texttt{proc\_peak\_phys\_mb}, extracted directly from Darwin XNU's \texttt{ri\_lifetime\_max\_phys\_footprint} counter in \texttt{RUSAGE\_INFO\_V4}) as our single primary metric for host memory consumption. Methodologically, \texttt{ri\_lifetime\_max\_phys\_footprint} measures the exact lifetime peak of anonymous dirty, wired, and compressed physical RAM pages exclusively allocated by the process—the exact quantity used by the Darwin kernel jetsam memory manager to enforce physical UMA limits—whereas POSIX \texttt{ru\_maxrss} (Peak RSS) reports the virtual resident set size, which conflates private heap allocations with clean, read-only system shared libraries (\texttt{dyld} shared cache) mapped across all processes. To prevent ambiguity, all architectural comparisons and decision rules in the text report \texttt{Peak Physical Footprint} (\texttt{proc\_peak\_phys\_mb}) exclusively.

\paragraph{Distinction Between Warm Process Execution and Warm KV-Cache Regime}
We explicitly distinguish between \textit{Warm Process Execution} and the \textit{Warm KV-Cache Regime (Regime B)}. In Warm Process Execution, client-side agent runtimes (e.g., Node.js V8 JIT compiler, CPython interpreter bytecode caches, or Go runtime threads) have completed initial binary loading, module imports, and heap allocation, eliminating cold interpreter bootstrap overheads (characterized separately via \texttt{hyperfine} cold-start profiling). In contrast, the Warm KV-Cache Regime (Regime B) specifically denotes that the server-side neural inference daemon (\texttt{llama-server}) retains pre-computed Key-Value attention states for recurring system prompts and tool schemas in GPU memory (\texttt{cache\_prompt=true}), avoiding redundant prompt attention prefill calculations across dialogue turns.

\subsection{Hardware Constraints, Permissions, and Two-Stage Energy Deconvolution}
In unified-memory System-on-Chip (SoC) architectures such as Apple Silicon M4, physical electrical shunt sensors managed by the System Management Controller (SMC) instrument aggregate hardware power rails (CPU clusters, Metal GPU, and ANE) at the package level rather than individual operating system processes \cite{barroso2007case,you2023zeus}. Consequently, isolating exclusive analog Joules for a single POSIX process during concurrent CPU--GPU execution is physically unobservable at the hardware shunt layer. Following established energy-benchmarking methodology for machine learning systems \cite{you2023zeus,georgiou2022energy,swenergy2025,greenbench2025}, our harness resolves this architectural constraint via a two-stage hardware validation protocol: (i) phase-isolated direct hardware measurement of client-side tool execution on the Apple SMC \texttt{CPU Power} rail ($E_{\text{CPU, net}}$) with the Metal GPU idle ($P_{\text{GPU}} \approx 0\text{ W}$) alongside paired baseline subtraction ($\Delta E_{\text{net}}$) against \texttt{neural\_baseline}, and (ii) empirical cross-validation of Darwin XNU \texttt{proc\_pid\_rusage} counters against physical SMC current shunts. Crucially, our empirical validation establishes a formal \textbf{Negative Result for Darwin XNU \texttt{ri\_billed\_energy}}: across 120 isolated CPU trials, uncalibrated \texttt{ri\_billed\_energy} exhibits zero global correlation with physical SMC \texttt{CPU Power} rail Joules (Pearson $r = -0.0510, p = 0.580$; centered $\text{OLS } R^2 = -0.676$ relative to the sample mean), performing worse than a constant mean predictor and overestimating CPython energy by $10\text{--}30\times$ due to internal XNU scheduler QoS/thread-weighting heuristics, directly corroborating prior findings by Fahad et al.~\cite{fahad2019comparative} on the unreliability of uncalibrated on-chip energy estimation models (which can incur $73\%$ average and up to $300\%$ maximum error). Conversely, physical duration (\texttt{t\_exec\_ms}, $r = 0.992, R^2 = 0.949$), retired CPU instructions (\texttt{proc\_instructions}, $r = 0.985, R^2 = 0.955$), and core cycles (\texttt{proc\_cycles}, $r = 0.991, R^2 = 0.945$) serve as rigorously validated physical CPU energy proxies.

Direct access to Apple SMC hardware sensors via \texttt{powermetrics} requires elevated \texttt{root} privileges due to Darwin XNU kernel protections against microarchitectural power side-channel vulnerabilities. Crucially, the benchmark runner enforces privilege isolation: all evaluated agent runtimes, orchestrator subprocesses, and the \texttt{llama-server} neural daemon execute strictly in unprivileged user space. When administrative privileges are absent, the telemetry layer degrades gracefully without synthetic estimation, recording latency, memory footprint, and \texttt{libproc} counters while marking electrical energy fields as unavailable (\texttt{N/A}).

\subsection{Analytical Finding: Cold Prefill Stall in W3 and Dispersion Dynamics}
Analysis of the 600 evaluated trial records ($n=30$ per condition) in \texttt{raw\_trials.jsonl} demonstrates the necessity of non-parametric statistics on unified memory architectures \cite{jain1991art,lilja2000measuring}. In vector similarity ranking (W3), PicoClaw exhibits an empirical median task latency of $\WThreePicoMedian\text{ ms}$ with an Interquartile Range ($\text{IQR}$) of $\WThreePicoIQR\text{ ms}$ (mean $\WThreePicoMean \pm \WThreePicoSD\text{ ms}$), compared to $\WThreeOpenMinMedian\text{ ms}$ ($\text{IQR} = \WThreeOpenMinIQR\text{ ms}$) for OpenClaw Minimal and $\WThreeOpenFullMedian\text{ ms}$ ($\text{IQR} = \WThreeOpenFullIQR\text{ ms}$) for OpenClaw Full. Stochastic dominance testing confirms PicoClaw significantly outperforms OpenClaw Minimal (Cliff's Delta $d = \WThreeCliffsDeltaMin$, $\hat{A}_{12} = \WThreeAOneTwoMin$, Holm-Bonferroni adjusted $p \WThreeAdjPMin$) and OpenClaw Full ($d = \WThreeCliffsDeltaFull$, $\hat{A}_{12} = \WThreeAOneTwoFull$, adjusted $p \WThreeAdjPFull$). Isolated orchestrator delay in W3 confirms Go runtime efficiency: PicoClaw incurs a median $T_{\text{orch}}$ of only $\WThreePicoOrchMedian\text{ ms}$ vs. $\WThreeOpenMinOrchMedian\text{ ms}$ for OpenClaw Minimal and $\WThreeOpenFullOrchMedian\text{ ms}$ for OpenClaw Full.

\subsection{Orchestration Overhead Isolation and Cross-Language Tool-Chain Dynamics in W2}
In the three-step tool chain workload (W2), aggregate physical task metrics demonstrate end-to-end latency parity between native and managed orchestrators: PicoClaw recorded a median task latency of $\WTwoPicoMedian\text{ ms}$ (mean $\WTwoPicoMean\text{ ms}$, $\WTwoPicoEnergy\text{ J}$) compared to $\WTwoOpenMinMedian\text{ ms}$ (mean $\WTwoOpenMinMean\text{ ms}$, $\WTwoOpenMinEnergy\text{ J}$) for OpenClaw Minimal, closely matching the direct NeuralBaseline ($\WTwoNeuralMedian\text{ ms}$, mean $\WTwoNeuralMean\text{ ms}$, $\WTwoNeuralEnergy\text{ J}$). Granular inspection of \texttt{raw\_trials.jsonl} confirms that under greedy autoregressive decoding with 15 standardized schema definitions, the underlying LLaMA-3.2-3B model emitted an identical token count of $\WTwoPicoTokens$ tokens for PicoClaw ($T_{\text{neural}} = \WTwoNeuralPico\text{ ms}$) and $\WTwoOpenMinTokens$ tokens for OpenClaw Minimal ($T_{\text{neural}} = \WTwoNeuralOpenMin\text{ ms}$), achieving complete end-to-end parity within $1\text{ ms}$ ($5556.1\text{ ms}$ vs. $5557.1\text{ ms}$).

Crucially, when isolating runtime orchestrator overhead ($T_{\text{orch}}$) and separating client-side dispatch delay ($T_{\text{dispatch}}$) from HTTP transport latency ($T_{\text{http}}$), the architectural characteristics of the three language runtimes become clearly measurable:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Go Native (\texttt{PicoClaw}):} Achieves the lowest median orchestrator delay of $T_{\text{orch}} = \WTwoPicoOrchMedian\text{ ms}$ (mean $\WTwoPicoOrchMean\text{ ms}$), combining $T_{\text{dispatch}} = 1.78\text{ ms}$ with $T_{\text{http}} \approx 3.80\text{ ms}$ powered by Go's native \texttt{net/http} stack and non-blocking goroutines.
    \item \textbf{CPython Reference (\texttt{PythonNative}):} Incurs an intermediate median overhead of $T_{\text{orch}} = 11.23\text{ ms}$ ($T_{\text{dispatch}} = 1.00\text{ ms}$, $T_{\text{http}} \approx 10.23\text{ ms}$), reflecting synchronous socket I/O in Python's standard \texttt{urllib.request}.
    \item \textbf{Node.js V8 (\texttt{OpenClaw Minimal}):} Registers a median $T_{\text{orch}} = \WTwoOpenMinOrchMedian\text{ ms}$ (mean $\WTwoOpenMinOrchMean\text{ ms}$), where dispatch requires $1.31\text{ ms}$ while the V8 asynchronous event loop and Undici/\texttt{fetch} stream pipeline add $19.64\text{ ms}$ of socket buffer translation and GC book-keeping.
\end{itemize}
Overall, Go achieves $\WTwoOrchSpeedup\times$ lower orchestrator latency ($T_{\text{orch}}$) than Node.js Minimal. Furthermore, in physical DRAM allocation measured via Darwin kernel \texttt{libproc} (\texttt{proc\_peak\_phys\_mb}, \texttt{ri\_lifetime\_max\_phys\_footprint}), PicoClaw required a peak physical footprint of merely $5.07\text{ MB}$ ($5.1\text{ MB}$ in Table~\ref{tab:evaluation_ablation_m4}) in W2 versus $23.69\text{ MB}$ ($23.7\text{ MB}$) for Python and $37.69\text{ MB}$ ($37.7\text{ MB}$) for Node.js V8 ($7.43\times$ lower physical DRAM footprint in W2). Token-normalized physical energy consumption confirms near-identical efficiency ($\WTwoPicoJoulePerToken\text{ J/token}$ for PicoClaw vs. $\WTwoOpenMinJoulePerToken\text{ J/token}$ for OpenClaw Minimal), demonstrating that client-side orchestrator choice dictates host resource utilization ($\text{CPU cycles}$, $\text{instructions}$, and $\text{Peak Physical Footprint}$) rather than GPU energy saturation during prompt prefill. To guarantee full scientific provenance and auditability across revisions, ISO-8601 timestamps embedded within the canonical \texttt{raw\_trials.jsonl} dataset rigorously distinguish the original experimental session for W1, W3, and W4 (recorded between \texttt{2026-11-12T03:38:57Z} and \texttt{05:15:00Z}, encompassing 495 trials) from the focused W2 resampling session (recorded between \texttt{2026-11-12T14:20:00Z} and \texttt{14:34:54Z}, encompassing 165 trials following client-side SSE streaming parser standardization and compact tool schema equalization).

\subsection{Controlled Tool Catalog Schema Prefill and KV-Cache Reuse Characterization (\texttt{llama.cpp} / Metal GPU)}
To address the critical distinction between orchestrator-induced dispatch delay ($T_{\text{orch}}$) and prompt-prefill attention computation ($T_{\text{prefill}}$) on unified memory architectures \cite{kwon2023vllm,gim2024promptcache,zheng2024sglang,patel2024splitwise,reddi2020mlperf,llmperf2023}, we conducted a controlled schema ablation directly against the local inference engine (\texttt{llama.cpp}) across 8 tool catalog sizes $T \in \{0, 5, 10, 15, 25, 40, 54, 100\}$ and prompt-cache configurations ($\texttt{cache\_prompt} \in \{\text{false}, \text{true}\}$, $n=30$ independent physical evaluations per cell following 3 priming warmups). This controlled experiment explicitly characterizes the prefill scaling behavior of the underlying Metal GPU inference server under progressive tool schema injection ($0$ to $100$ schemas), providing an empirical baseline for the impact of prompt bloat independently of host orchestrator execution. The consolidated empirical results are presented in Table~\ref{tab:prefill_ablation}.

\input{../results/publication_campaign/tables/table_prefill_ablation.tex}

As documented in Table~\ref{tab:prefill_ablation}, in-memory schema serialization and dispatch delay ($T_{\text{dispatch}}$) remains negligible ($< 2\text{ ms}$). However, un-cached prompt prefill latency ($T_{\text{prefill}}$) on the Metal GPU scales steeply with catalog dimension: prefill latency increases from approximately $128\text{ ms}$ at $0$ tools ($60$ tokens) to over $4{,}400\text{ ms}$ at $15$ tools ($2{,}081$ tokens), $17{,}900\text{ ms}$ at $54$ tools ($7{,}190$ tokens), and reaching nearly $39{,}000\text{ ms}$ at $100$ tools ($13{,}216$ prompt tokens), dominating total execution duration.

Regression analysis over the empirical trials in \texttt{prefill\_ablation.jsonl} confirms super-linear scaling: a power-law fit ($T_{\text{prefill}} = 0.3197 \cdot N^{1.2342}$ with 95\% CI $\alpha \in [1.2324, 1.2360]$, $R^2 = 0.9993$, $\text{AIC}_c = 99.25$) and quadratic model ($T_{\text{prefill}} = 7.895 \times 10^{-5} N^2 + 1.9201 N + 17.36\text{ ms}$, $R^2 = 0.99999$, $\text{AIC}_c = 68.88$) decisively outperform linear prefill approximations ($R^2 = 0.9889$, $\text{AIC}_c = 120.84$), reflecting the combined super-linear prefill cost of transformer self-attention, prompt batching (\texttt{ubatch}), and Metal kernel scheduling in \texttt{llama.cpp} prior to KV-cache allocation (where the narrow bootstrap CI $\alpha \in [1.2324, 1.2360]$ reflects intra-session curve-fit precision). Crucially, activating KV-cache reuse ($\texttt{cache\_prompt} = \text{true}$) bypasses redundant attention evaluations across identical schema prefixes, unlocking a massive $5.5\times$ to $946.5\times$ speedup on prefill latency (reducing TTFT to sub-second levels across all tool catalog dimensions). This empirical characterization confirms that stock enterprise multi-tool agent schemas impair execution primarily through super-linear neural attention prefill scaling rather than host orchestrator delay \cite{kwon2023vllm,gim2024promptcache,zheng2024sglang,patel2024splitwise,schick2023toolformer,kvflow2025,rrevict2025,efficientagent2025,prefixbench2025,recache2025}.

To provide an active software-level solution to enterprise schema bloat alongside server-side KV-cache retention, we implemented and evaluated a \textbf{Zero-Copy Dynamic Tool-RAG Schema Pruner} (\texttt{OPENCLAW\_MODE="tool\_rag"}) inside the Go, Node.js V8, and Python orchestrator workers, motivated by recent advances in dynamic tool and MCP pruning (\textit{ToolGap} \cite{toolgap2025}, \textit{ProMCP} \cite{promcp2025}, \textit{Nexus} \cite{nexus2025}, and \textit{SMELT} \cite{smelt2025}). Prior to HTTP payload serialization, the orchestrator initializes the full 54-tool corporate catalog ($N_{\text{schemas}} = 54$) and executes an in-memory 384-dimensional cosine similarity dot-product against the workload query embedding to select only the $\text{Top-}K$ ($K=5$) semantically relevant tools. Table~\ref{tab:tool_rag_mitigation} reports the physical evaluation across W1--W4 under both Regime A and Regime B ($n=30$ physical trials per condition with continuous SMC power telemetry). Semantic pruning adds merely $0.033\text{ to }0.038\text{ ms}$ per turn in Go ($0.382\text{ ms}$ across 10 turns in W4) and $0.286\text{ to }0.427\text{ ms}$ per turn in Node.js V8 ($3.330\text{--}4.268\text{ ms}$ across 10 turns in W4) while shrinking the serialized prompt from $7{,}067\text{--}7{,}499$ tokens to $893$ tokens in W1 ($72{,}120\text{--}76{,}440$ to $9{,}197\text{--}10{,}380$ tokens across 10 turns in W4). Under cold prefill (Regime A), active Tool-RAG pruning eliminates $85.5\%\text{ to }87.2\%$ of end-to-end latency and $85.6\%\text{ to }87.8\%$ of net electrical energy relative to the unpruned 54-tool catalog (and reduces latency by $50.9\%\text{ to }54.6\%$ even relative to the static 15-tool catalog), while in Regime B it reduces active KV-cache attention span by $21.1\%\text{ to }37.9\%$ vs. 54 tools, outperforming even static 15-tool execution ($952.4\text{ ms}$ vs. $1033.5\text{ ms}$ in W1; $8334.2\text{--}8358.8\text{ ms}$ vs. $8605.5\text{ ms}$ in W4).

\subsection{Comparative Analysis: Cold Prefill Regime vs. Realistic KV-Cache Reuse Regime}
To evaluate runtime performance under production deployment scenarios where common tool catalogs and system prompts persist across agent turns, we conducted a companion campaign of 600 evaluated physical trials (60 priming warmups excluded) under active KV-cache prefix retention (\texttt{cache\_prompt=true}, $n=30$ independent physical evaluations per condition, block-isolated to eliminate single-slot cache thrashing in \texttt{llama-server}). The empirical results are presented in Table~\ref{tab:evaluation_warm_cache_m4}.

\input{../results/publication_campaign/tables/table_warm_cache.tex}

Contrasting the cold prefill regime (Regime A, Table~\ref{tab:evaluation_ablation_m4}) and the warm cache regime (Regime B, Table~\ref{tab:evaluation_warm_cache_m4}) reveals the operational trade-offs of edge agent execution:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regime A (\texttt{cache\_prompt=false}):} Cold prompt attention prefill on the Metal GPU accounts for $>75\%$ of end-to-end task latency across all configurations. Because the GPU saturates package power at $\approx 16\text{ W}$ during attention computation, total latency and active energy remain dominated by neural matrix evaluation, producing near-parity in wall-clock duration between host runtimes.
    \item \textbf{Regime B (\texttt{cache\_prompt=true}) and Physical KV-Cache Thrashing vs. Slot Isolation Ablation:} When prompt prefill is amortized through verified block-isolated KV-cache prefix retention in \texttt{llama-server} ($\text{Cache Hit}$ of $99.9\%$ to $100.0\%$ in W1--W3 and $97.7\%$ to $99.3\%$ in W4; 20 randomized condition blocks with 3 priming warmups and 30 consecutive evaluations per block in single-slot \texttt{-np 1}), overall task duration drops dramatically: in W2, task latency falls to sub-second levels ($741.2\text{ ms}$ for Go Native, $743.4\text{ ms}$ for Python 3.12, $752.8\text{ ms}$ for Node.js Minimal, and $937.7\text{ ms}$ for Node.js Full---a $21.1\times$ reduction for the 54-tool catalog compared to un-cached cold execution). Table~\ref{tab:evaluation_warm_cache_m4} explicitly decomposes total prompt tokens into cached prefix tokens and newly evaluated uncached tokens (\texttt{Tot / Cached / New}) alongside median \texttt{TTFT / ITL}, characterizing five fundamental KV-cache operational behaviors: (i) \textit{cold cache} (\texttt{cache\_prompt=false}), where all $N_{\text{prompt}}$ tokens undergo full attention evaluation ($\texttt{cached\_tokens}=0$, $\text{TTFT} > 4.4\text{ s}$); (ii) \textit{warm cache with identical prefix} (\texttt{cache\_prompt=true} intra-block), where consecutive trials reuse $99.9\%$ of the prefix ($2{,}152 / 2{,}153$ in W1, $2{,}176 / 2{,}177$ in W2, $2{,}409 / 2{,}410$ in W3) and evaluate only $\texttt{New}=1$ boundary token ($\text{TTFT} = 232.4\text{--}269.5\text{ ms}$, $\text{ITL} = 16.24\text{--}20.14\text{ ms}$); (iii) \textit{prefix switching across condition blocks}, where transitioning between 15-tool, 54-tool, or Top-5 schemas triggers a prefix miss absorbed by the 3 priming warmups; (iv) \textit{interleaved prefix thrashing vs. isolated slot retention} (\texttt{scripts/run\_kv\_cache\_thrashing\_ablation.py}, archived in \texttt{kv\_cache\_thrashing\_trials.jsonl} and \texttt{kv\_cache\_thrashing\_summary.json}, $n=30$ physical evaluations per regime in W2): when alternating every request between two unisolated agents with distinct system headers and tool catalogs (15 schemas [$2{,}177$ tokens] vs. 54 schemas [$7{,}091$ tokens]) sharing a single KV-cache slot (\texttt{-np 1}, Regime T2), the active prefix diverges right after the 32-token template preamble ($\texttt{cached\_tokens} = 31.9\text{--}32.0$, $\text{Cache Hit} = 0.96\%$ overall and $0.45\%$ on 54 schemas vs. $99.95\%\text{--}99.99\%$ under isolated prefix retention in Regime T3), inflicting a $7.37\times$ task latency penalty on the 15-tool agent ($5472.9\text{ ms}$ vs. $742.3\text{ ms}$) and a $19.91\times$ task latency penalty on the 54-tool agent ($18551.4\text{ ms}$ vs. $931.9\text{ ms}$, corresponding to a $531.3\times$ prefill latency penalty [$17613.6\text{ ms}$ vs. $33.15\text{ ms}$] and escalating mean net energy from $11.51\text{ J}$ to $313.07\text{ J}$); and (v) \textit{prefix invalidation and multi-turn growth} in W4, where cumulative 10-turn tokens reach $22{,}980$ ($22{,}457$ cached [$97.7\%$] and $523$ newly evaluated [$\approx 52.3\text{ tokens/turn}$]). Across W1--W4, Go Native, Python 3.12, and Node.js Minimal remain within $-0.92\%$ to $+5.83\%$ in median ($-1.14\%$ to $+5.85\%$ in mean; and within $-0.92\%$ to $+1.79\%$ across W1, W2, and W4) of the Direct Local LLM Baseline ($739.6\text{ ms}$ median in W2; $8503.6\text{ ms}$ in W4). Crucially, under this amortized prefill regime, host orchestrator overhead is no longer masked by GPU attention stalls, elevating the relative orchestration share ($\Phi_{\text{orch}} = T_{\text{orch}} / T_{\text{task}}$) to clearly measurable intervals between $0.37\%$ and $4.65\%$ ($T_{\text{dispatch}} = 1.78\text{ ms}$ and $T_{\text{http}} = 3.36\text{ ms}$ for Go; $T_{\text{dispatch}} = 1.04\text{ ms}$ and $T_{\text{http}} = 9.85\text{ ms}$ for Python; $T_{\text{dispatch}} = 1.25\text{ ms}$ and $T_{\text{http}} = 19.02\text{ ms}$ for Node.js Minimal in W2), demonstrating that language runtime efficiency becomes directly measurable in I/O and vector-intensive workloads (W2--W3) while remaining within server timing noise ($\pm 1\text{--}2\%$) in W1 and W4. Furthermore, formal sensitivity and outlier analysis (\texttt{sensitivity\_and\_outlier\_analysis.json}) applying pre-registered Tukey fences ($[Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}, Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}]$) and $5\%$ trimmed means across all 40 experimental blocks ($1{,}200$ trials) confirms that only $2.67\%$ ($16/600$) of Regime A trials and $1.83\%$ ($11/600$) of Regime B trials are Tukey outliers ($\le 3.3\%$), and removing all outliers shifts cell medians by at most $0.075\%$ in Regime A and $0.094\%$ in Regime B ($< 0.8\%$), proving that no statistical conclusion depends on extreme observations; simultaneously, active electrical power decomposition ($\bar{P}_{\text{net}} = E_{\text{net}} / T_{\text{task}}$) separates high-power GPU attention prefill in Regime A ($\bar{P}_{\text{net}} = 15.86 \pm 0.40\text{ W}$, median $\texttt{prompt\_ms} = 5331.9\text{ ms}$ vs. $\texttt{predicted\_ms} = 718.0\text{ ms}$) from bandwidth-bound autoregressive decode in Regime B ($\bar{P}_{\text{net}} = 13.04 \pm 0.81\text{ W}$, median $\texttt{prompt\_ms} = 245.5\text{ ms}$ vs. $\texttt{predicted\_ms} = 774.7\text{ ms}$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Key Findings, Hardware Microarchitecture, and Effect Sizes}
Across all 600 physical trials on the Apple Silicon M4 testbed, three core empirical conclusions emerge:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Microarchitectural Efficiency:} Hardware performance counters captured via Darwin XNU kernel \texttt{libproc} reveal that PicoClaw executes an average of $\PicoInstructionsMeanM \times 10^6$ retired CPU instructions and $\PicoCyclesMeanM \times 10^6$ CPU cycles, compared to $\OpenMinInstructionsMeanM \times 10^6$ instructions ($\InstructionOverheadRatio\times$ overhead) and $\OpenMinCyclesMeanM \times 10^6$ cycles ($\CyclesOverheadRatio\times$ overhead) for OpenClaw Minimal.
    \item \textbf{Physical DRAM Footprint Reduction (\texttt{Peak Physical Footprint}):} Lifetime peak physical footprint (\texttt{proc\_peak\_phys\_mb}, extracted from Darwin XNU's \texttt{ri\_lifetime\_max\_phys\_footprint}) confirms that PicoClaw allocates only $4.2\text{--}45.5\text{ MB}$ across W1--W4 (averaging $\PicoPeakPhysMean\text{ MB}$ overall, rising to $45.5\text{ MB}$ only during 10,000-vector cosine dot products in W3), compared to $37.5\text{--}67.1\text{ MB}$ (averaging $\OpenMinPeakPhysMean\text{ MB}$) for Node.js V8---a $\PeakPhysReductionMinRatio\times$ to $\PeakPhysReductionMaxRatio\times$ reduction in physical DRAM allocation ($\PeakPhysLeanerRatio\times$ leaner on global average).
    \item \textbf{Tool Catalog Ablation and EDP Efficiency (Dual-Regime Dynamics):} Evaluating 54 enterprise schemas (OpenClaw Full) against 15 schemas (Minimal) reveals severe prompt bloat: W1 mean latency balloons from $\WOneOpenMinMean\text{ ms}$ to $\WOneOpenFullMean\text{ ms}$, while mean energy escalates from $\WOneOpenMinEnergy\text{ J}$ to $\WOneOpenFullEnergy\text{ J}$ ($\WOneEnergyBloatRatio\times$). In the 10-turn autonomous ReAct loop (W4) under Regime A, OpenClaw Full expends $\WFourOpenFullEnergy\text{ J}$ with an $\text{EDP}$ of $\WFourOpenFullEDP\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$ ($\WFourOpenFullMedian\text{ ms}$ median), whereas PicoClaw completes W4 in $\WFourPicoMedian\text{ ms}$ median with an $\text{EDP}$ of $\WFourPicoEDP\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$ ($\WFourEDPReductionPct\%$ lower EDP), demonstrating significant stochastic effect sizes ($d = \WFourCliffsDelta$, $\hat{A}_{12} = \WFourAOneTwo$, adjusted $p \WFourAdjP$) by avoiding 54-tool catalog prompt bloat. Under Regime B with amortized prefill (unified single-session collection across all 5 arms in W2 and W4, with the earlier two-session collection preserved in \texttt{warm\_cache\_trials\_two\_session\_backup.jsonl} for full auditability), equalized 15-tool arms converge closely in latency and EDP ($1027.76\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$ for Go Native, $1004.28\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$ for Python 3.12, and $967.24\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$ for Node.js Minimal) while remaining $41.6\%\text{ to }45.1\%$ lower in EDP than the unpruned 54-tool catalog ($1760.40\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$). Notably, while CPython records higher raw kernel \texttt{proc\_billed\_energy} units ($84{,}819$ vs. $7{,}050$ XNU units for Go in W4), our isolated SMC rail validation (Section~\ref{sec:energy_bandwidth_deconvolution}) proves that \texttt{ri\_billed\_energy} is an uncalibrated scheduler QoS proxy ($r = -0.0510$) rather than a valid Joule estimator.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Two-Stage Process Energy Deconvolution and Analytical \& Empirical Memory Bandwidth Estimation}
\label{sec:energy_bandwidth_deconvolution}
To isolate per-process orchestrator energy on Apple Silicon Unified Memory Architecture (UMA) where electrical shunts instrument aggregate package rails, we executed a two-stage hardware characterization protocol combining (i) phase-isolated tool benchmarking directly on the Apple SMC \texttt{CPU Power} rail with GPU idle alongside paired baseline subtraction ($\Delta E_{\text{net}}$) against \texttt{neural\_baseline}, and (ii) empirical validation of Darwin XNU kernel \texttt{proc\_pid\_rusage} counters against physical SMC Joules. Table~\ref{tab:energy_bandwidth_deconvolution} reports the empirical findings across 120 isolated CPU trials and deconvolution over 1,200 evaluated campaign trials.

\input{../results/publication_campaign/tables/table_energy_bandwidth_deconvolution.tex}

Direct physical measurement on the Apple SMC \texttt{CPU Power} rail during isolated local dispatch with the Metal GPU quiescent ($P_{\text{GPU}} \approx 0\text{ W}$) captures the true marginal electrical cost of host orchestration (reported with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals over 2,000 resamples): in the 3-tool chain (W2), compiled Go consumes $1.252\text{ mJ/op}$ (95\% CI $[1.224, 1.280]$), CPython consumes $1.000\text{ mJ/op}$ ($[0.961, 1.041]$), and Node.js V8 requires $0.196\text{ mJ/op}$ ($[0.192, 0.200]$ Minimal) and $0.178\text{ mJ/op}$ ($[0.175, 0.181]$ Full). In vector similarity search (W3, 10,000 documents), compiled Go consumes $136.62\text{ mJ/op}$ ($[134.97, 138.30]$), OpenClaw Minimal requires $45.67\text{ mJ/op}$ ($[44.43, 46.97]$), OpenClaw Full requires $44.36\text{ mJ/op}$ ($[43.52, 45.22]$), and Python/NumPy consumes $34.08\text{ mJ/op}$ ($[31.66, 36.61]$). Crucially, cross-validating Darwin XNU's \texttt{ri\_billed\_energy} counter against physical SMC \texttt{CPU Power} rail Joules ($E_{\text{CPU, net}}$) across all 120 isolated trials establishes a formal \textbf{Negative Result}: \texttt{ri\_billed\_energy} exhibits zero global linear correlation with true CPU energy ($r = -0.0510, p = 0.580$; intra-agent $r = -0.10$ for Go, $-0.08$ for Python, and $+0.07$ for Node.js Minimal) and a negative centered coefficient of determination ($\text{OLS } R^2 = -0.676$ vs. the sample mean), proving that uncalibrated XNU billing units perform worse than predicting the mean and overestimate CPython energy by $10\text{--}30\times$ due to internal XNU scheduler QoS/thread-weighting heuristics, corroborating Fahad et al.~\cite{fahad2019comparative}. Consequently, we reject \texttt{ri\_billed\_energy} as a Joule estimator and establish three validated physical CPU energy regressors: execution duration (\texttt{t\_exec\_ms}: $r = 0.992, \text{OLS } R^2 = 0.949$; intra-agent $\text{corr}(t, E_{\text{CPU, net}}) = 1.00$ for Go, $0.99$ for Node-Minimal, and $0.85$ for Python), retired CPU instructions (\texttt{proc\_instructions}: $r = 0.985, \text{OLS } R^2 = 0.955$), and core cycles (\texttt{proc\_cycles}: $r = 0.991, \text{OLS } R^2 = 0.945$). Using exclusively the direct physical SMC \texttt{CPU Power} rail dispatch energy ($E_{\text{CPU, net}}^{\text{dispatch}}$) divided by Regime B net task energy ($E_{\text{net, task}}$):
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{energy, orch}} = \frac{E_{\text{CPU, net}}^{\text{dispatch}}}{E_{\text{net, task}}}
\end{equation}
we confirm that under amortized KV-cache reuse (Regime B), true host orchestrator CPU energy represents merely $0.04\% / 1.42\%$ ($3.76\text{ mJ} / 8.86\text{ J}$ in W2; $136.62\text{ mJ} / 9.64\text{ J}$ in W3) for PicoClaw (Go Native), $0.03\% / 0.34\%$ ($3.00\text{ mJ} / 9.70\text{ J}$ in W2; $34.08\text{ mJ} / 9.92\text{ J}$ in W3) for PythonNative, $0.01\% / 0.49\%$ ($0.59\text{ mJ} / 9.95\text{ J}$ in W2; $45.67\text{ mJ} / 9.38\text{ J}$ in W3) for OpenClaw Minimal, and $0.01\% / 0.30\%$ ($0.53\text{ mJ} / 11.00\text{ J}$ in W2; $44.36\text{ mJ} / 14.74\text{ J}$ in W3) for OpenClaw Full.

Analytical and empirical memory bandwidth estimation across the unified 120~GB/s LPDDR5X bus is modeled via:
\begin{equation}
\text{BW}_{\text{host, W3}} = \frac{S_{\text{RAG}}}{T_{\text{RAG}}}, \quad \text{BW}_{\text{decode}} = \frac{S_{\text{weights}} + N_{\text{ctx}} \cdot S_{\text{KV/tok}}}{\text{ITL}_{\text{decode}}}
\end{equation}
In host vector search (W3), streaming the $S_{\text{RAG}} = 15.75\text{ MB}$ projection weights and document embeddings achieves $0.67\text{ GB/s}$ in Go, $1.80\text{ GB/s}$ in Node.js Minimal ($1.82\text{ GB/s}$ in Full), and $2.04\text{ GB/s}$ in Python. During autoregressive neural decoding on the Metal GPU (`batch_size=1`), assuming one complete read of the $S_{\text{weights}} = 2.019\text{ GB}$ GGUF Q4\_K\_M weight tensors per generated token divided by pure inter-token decode latency ($\text{ITL}_{\text{decode}} = \texttt{predicted\_ms} / \texttt{predicted\_n}$), our analytical-empirical estimation yields a global median memory bandwidth of $97.0\text{ GB/s}$ ($80.8\%$ of nominal $120\text{ GB/s}$) and a mean ranging from $95.9\text{ GB/s}$ ($79.9\%$ in Regime A, 95\% bootstrap CI $[95.6, 96.2]\text{ GB/s}$, IQR $6.0\text{ GB/s}$) to $106.7\text{ GB/s}$ ($88.9\%$ in Regime B, 95\% bootstrap CI $[106.4, 107.1]\text{ GB/s}$, IQR $8.0\text{ GB/s}$; global mean $104.0\text{ GB/s}$ / $86.7\%$). We emphasize that this represents an analytical-empirical bandwidth estimation ($\text{Model Weights} \div \text{ITL}$) rather than hardware DRAM controller counter telemetry, and its observed $80.8\%\text{--}88.9\%$ utilization aligns closely with the practical Roofline memory bus saturation ceiling ($\approx 86\text{--}88\%$) established for Apple Silicon UMA architectures \cite{imamura2026apple,benazir2025llm}.

\section{Impact}
\label{sec:impact}
This benchmark suite provides the research and software engineering communities with a reproducible, modular testbed to isolate and quantify AI agent orchestration runtime overhead. By decoupling orchestrator dispatch and schema validation delays from neural model latency, the suite enables systems researchers to systematically evaluate language runtime trade-offs (compiled Go, JIT Node.js, and CPython) under realistic tool-use loops \cite{yao2023react,schick2023toolformer,lewis2020retrieval}. The framework's modular adapter architecture facilitates adding new agent runtimes or language environments (e.g., Rust, Zig, or Julia) with fewer than 40 lines of code. Furthermore, the dual-tier telemetry approach---combining unprivileged kernel performance counters with non-intrusive Apple SMC and Linux RAPL electrical shunt sampling---provides an extensible blueprint for benchmarking edge AI systems under strict power and thermal budgets \cite{georgiou2022energy,you2023zeus,barroso2007case}.

\subsection{Empirical Architectural Decision Rules}
Based on our multi-regime empirical benchmarks across compiled, JIT-interpreted, and bytecode runtimes on Apple Silicon Unified Memory Architecture (UMA), we synthesize four objective architectural decision rules for edge agent deployment:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ephemeral CLI / Cold-Start Invocation:} For short-lived CLI utilities, serverless functions, or bursty edge triggers, native compiled binaries (Go Mach-O) provide a $3.81\times$ faster mean startup latency ($9.74\text{ ms}$ vs. $37.15\text{ ms}$ for Node.js V8; median $3.46\times$, $9.67\text{ ms}$ vs. $33.48\text{ ms}$ in \texttt{hyperfine\_cold\_start.json}) and $7.29\times$ faster isomorphic worker initialization ($3.04\text{ ms}$ vs. $22.15\text{ ms}$ for Node.js and $49.32\text{ ms}$ for Python in \texttt{hyperfine\_worker\_version.json}), eliminating runtime interpreter bootstrap penalties.
    \item \textbf{Memory-Constrained Unified Memory (UMA) / Multi-Agent Edge:} On embedded devices and shared UMA SoCs where CPU and GPU contend for the same physical memory bus, Go native reduces peak physical DRAM allocation (\texttt{proc\_peak\_phys\_mb}, \texttt{ri\_lifetime\_max\_phys\_footprint}) by $\PeakPhysLeanerRatio\times$ on global average ($\PicoPeakPhysMean\text{ MB}$ vs. $\OpenMinPeakPhysMean\text{ MB}$ for Node.js V8; ranging from $4.2\text{--}45.5\text{ MB}$ in Go vs. $37.5\text{--}67.1\text{ MB}$ in Node.js V8, or $\PeakPhysReductionMinRatio\times$ to $\PeakPhysReductionMaxRatio\times$ across workloads). This safeguards vital physical DRAM capacity for model weights and KV-cache page tables.
    \item \textbf{Persistent Daemons with Warm KV-Cache and Compact Schemas ($\le 15$ tools):} When running persistent services with verified prefix caching ($\text{Cache Hit} \ge 97.6\%$) and lean schemas, compiled Go, Python, Node.js V8, and native Bun (JavaScriptCore) differ by less than $-1.5\%$ to $+5.8\%$ from the direct neural baseline ($\Phi_{\text{orch}} \in [0.37\%, 4.65\%]$, $\Phi_{\text{energy, orch}} \in [0.01\%, 1.42\%]$). Because independent inter-session latency drift ($\sim 3\%\text{--}10\%$) exceeds these small intra-session differences in W1, W2, and W4, wall-clock task latency across runtimes is statistically indistinguishable across sessions (except in vector-heavy W3 where SIMD/BLAS acceleration beats scalar Go loops by $5.6\%\text{--}5.8\%$), allowing language selection to prioritize ecosystem maturity and host memory constraints.
    \item \textbf{Large Enterprise Tool Catalogs ($\ge 50$ tools):} When scaling to broad enterprise tool sets, the primary performance bottleneck shifts entirely to super-linear attention prefill on the GPU ($\alpha \approx 1.23$) rather than host orchestration. Mitigating this bottleneck strictly requires deterministic KV-cache slot isolation (yielding up to $21.6\times$ latency reduction) or enabling our built-in \textbf{Zero-Copy Dynamic Tool-RAG Schema Pruner} ($54 \rightarrow \text{Top-}5$ tools in $<0.35\text{ ms}$, eliminating $>85\%$ of cold-prefill latency and energy overhead).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Threats to Validity and Methodological Limitations}
\label{sec:threats_to_validity}
To ensure rigorous interpretation and prevent over-generalization of our empirical findings, we explicitly document six methodological boundaries and threats to validity:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Thermal and Inter-Session Drift Boundary:} Across multi-hour benchmark campaigns on passively/compactly cooled SoCs, ambient temperature variations and Metal GPU dynamic frequency scaling introduce an observed inter-session latency drift of $0.22\%$ to $3.32\%$ (mean $1.62\%$, pooled $\text{CV} = 1.15\%$ in controlled back-to-back replication, and up to $\approx 3\%\text{--}10.3\%$ across non-adjacent sessions). Consequently, we establish the formal decision boundary that end-to-end task latency ($T_{\text{task}}$) contrasts smaller than the inter-session drift threshold hold strictly \textit{intra-session} under randomized block interleaving (as in W1, W2, and W4 for 15-tool arms, where runtime differences remain within $-1.57\%$ to $+3.48\%$). Only effects that exceed the inter-session drift floor or operate purely on the host CPU---specifically W3 Vector RAG ($+5.41\%$ to $+5.85\%$ scalar Go penalty vs. SIMD/BLAS), 54-tool vs. 15-tool/Tool-RAG schema bloat ($>21\%\text{--}87\%$), and isolated host microarchitectural metrics ($T_{\text{orch}}$, \texttt{Peak Phys RAM}, retired CPU instructions, and core cycles)---generalize invariantly across sessions.
    \item \textbf{Model Scale and Quantization Dependence:} Our empirical evaluation employs \texttt{Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct} (\texttt{Q4\_K\_M}, $2.019\text{ GB}$ weights). Scaling to larger foundation models (8B--70B parameters) or higher-precision formats (Q8\_0/FP16) increases neural prefill and decode latency ($T_{\text{neural}}$) proportionally to weight volume and layer depth, which further compresses the relative orchestrator overhead fraction ($\Phi_{\text{orch}} = T_{\text{orch}} / T_{\text{task}}$) while leaving absolute client-side dispatch delay ($T_{\text{orch}}$) and resident DRAM footprint (\texttt{proc\_peak\_phys\_mb}) invariant.
    \item \textbf{Apple Silicon Unified Memory Architecture (UMA) Specifics:} All physical hardware measurements were conducted on an Apple Mac mini M4 featuring $24\text{ GB}$ of shared LPDDR5X UMA memory ($120\text{ GB/s}$ theoretical peak). Unlike discrete PCIe GPU topologies on x86\_64 servers---where host-to-device DMA transfers across the PCIe bus incur explicit copy latency and separate DRAM/VRAM allocation---UMA shares a single physical memory pool between CPU and GPU. Consequently, client-side orchestrator heap bloat on UMA directly reduces the physical DRAM available for neural KV-cache slots and model weights.
    \item \textbf{KV-Cache Slot Isolation and Prefix Invalidation Protocol:} Regime B evaluates deterministic single-slot execution (\texttt{-np 1}) with block-isolated priming (3 warmups + 30 consecutive trials per condition) to measure steady-state prefix reuse ($\text{Cache Hit} = 97.7\%\text{--}100.0\%$). In multi-tenant or interleaved multi-agent servers where distinct system prompts contend for a single KV-cache slot without RadixAttention tree sharing \cite{zheng2024sglang} or multi-slot partitioning (\texttt{-np > 1}), frequent prefix invalidation degrades execution toward Regime A cold-prefill behavior.
    \item \textbf{Indirect Process Energy Attribution and Negative Validation of \texttt{ri\_billed\_energy}:} Because hardware power shunts on UMA SoCs instrument aggregate package rails rather than individual POSIX PIDs, concurrent CPU--GPU execution cannot be physically separated at the analog sensor level \cite{fahad2019comparative,you2023zeus}. Our phase-isolated calibration on the Apple SMC \texttt{CPU Power} rail formally refutes Darwin XNU's \texttt{ri\_billed\_energy} counter as a valid physical Joule proxy ($r = -0.0510, p = 0.580$, centered $\text{OLS } R^2 = -0.676$), demonstrating that uncalibrated kernel billing units reflect QoS scheduler heuristics rather than true energy. Accurate host energy attribution strictly requires either phase-isolated SMC \texttt{CPU Power} rail integration with the GPU idle ($\Phi_{\text{energy, orch}} \in [0.01\%, 1.42\%]$) or our validated physical regressors (\texttt{t\_exec\_ms}, \texttt{proc\_instructions}, and \texttt{proc\_cycles}, $R^2 = 0.945\text{--}0.955$).
    \item \textbf{Cross-Hardware Generalization:} While the software harness provides cross-platform support for Linux Intel RAPL (\texttt{powercap}) and portable dry-run verification (\texttt{make dry-run}), the empirical constants reported here ($\alpha = 1.2342$ prefill exponent, $97.0\text{ GB/s}$ median analytical-empirical decode bandwidth) are specific to the Apple M4 Metal backend and \texttt{llama.cpp b11887}. Future work should replicate these campaigns across higher-bandwidth Apple Silicon tiers (M4 Pro/Max/Ultra), discrete NVIDIA CUDA/NVML systems, and low-power ARM edge boards.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
The \texttt{picoclaw-vs-openclaw-benchmark} framework delivers an open, extensible methodology and active schema-pruning toolkit for systems-level evaluation of AI agent orchestrators on unified memory microarchitectures. Our empirical findings demonstrate that under equalized tool schemas and equivalent generation token counts, end-to-end task latency across Go, Node.js V8, native Bun (JavaScriptCore), and Python exhibits near-parity within sessions ($< 6\%$ variance) as package power saturates near 16~W on the Metal GPU, while independent inter-session replication ($n=30$) confirms that cross-session thermal/clock drift ($\sim 3\%\text{--}10\%$) exceeds intra-session runtime differences in W1, W2, and W4 (with W3 vector RAG favoring SIMD/BLAS over scalar Go loops by $5.6\%\text{--}5.8\%$). Crucially, native compiled Go execution provides decisive, session-invariant host microarchitectural advantages over managed V8 JIT and CPython runtimes: executing $\InstructionOverheadRatio\times$ fewer retired CPU instructions, consuming $\CyclesOverheadRatio\times$ fewer CPU cycles, launching $3.81\times$ faster in CLI cold-start ($7.29\times$ in minimal worker initialization), and maintaining a $\PeakPhysReductionMinRatio\times$ to $\PeakPhysReductionMaxRatio\times$ lower physical DRAM footprint. Furthermore, our hardware validation refutes uncalibrated XNU \texttt{ri\_billed\_energy} counters as a Joule proxy ($r = -0.0510, R^2 = -0.676$) in favor of direct SMC \texttt{CPU Power} rail isolation ($\Phi_{\text{energy, orch}} \le 1.42\%$) and validated instruction/time regressors ($R^2 \ge 0.949$), while our active Zero-Copy Tool-RAG Schema Pruner eliminates $>85\%$ of 54-tool catalog prefill bloat in under $0.35\text{ ms}$. Through standardized workload tiers, rigorous non-parametric statistics, and portable reviewer dry-runs, this software establishes a foundation for evaluating next-generation edge intelligence.

# Cover Letter for Submission to SoftwareX (Elsevier)

**To:**  
The Editor-in-Chief  
*SoftwareX*, Elsevier

---

Dear Editor-in-Chief and Editorial Board of *SoftwareX*,

I am pleased to submit our original software manuscript titled **"A Reproducible Benchmark Suite for Isolating Orchestration Overhead, Schema Prefill Scaling, and KV-Cache Dynamics in Local LLM Agents on Apple Silicon"** for consideration as an original research software article in *SoftwareX*.

### Originality and Scientific Contribution
With the rapid migration of autonomous AI agents from remote cloud endpoints to local client devices, execution runtime bottlenecks have become a defining constraint. On unified memory architectures such as Apple Silicon (UMA @ 120 GB/s), agent orchestrators compete directly with quantized neural weights (`llama.cpp`) for memory bus bandwidth and compute cycles.

Our software, `picoclaw-vs-openclaw-benchmark`, addresses this fundamental challenge by providing both an extensible systems benchmarking harness and an active schema-pruning module:
1. **Mathematical Isolation of Orchestrator Overhead ($T_{\text{orch}}$) and Active Zero-Copy Tool-RAG Schema Pruner:** A modular framework decoupling neural inference latency from agent execution delay across four standardized workload tiers (W1–W4) and four language runtimes (Go Mach-O ARM64, Node.js V8, native Bun/JavaScriptCore, and CPython 3.12). Furthermore, the software includes an active **Zero-Copy Dynamic Tool-RAG Schema Pruner** ($54 \rightarrow \text{Top-}5$ semantically relevant tools via 384-D in-memory cosine similarity in $<0.35\text{ ms}$) that eliminates $>85\%$ of enterprise 54-tool prefill latency and energy under cold execution and outperforms static 15-tool schemas under warm KV-cache retention.
2. **Two-Stage Hardware Telemetry, XNU Counter Negative-Result Validation, and Analytical-Empirical UMA Bandwidth Estimation:** Physical instrumentation combining (i) direct power rail sampling on the Apple SMC `CPU Power` rail with GPU idle and paired baseline subtraction against `neural_baseline` with (ii) per-process microarchitectural counters from Darwin XNU (`ri_instructions`, `ri_cycles`, and `ri_billed_energy`). Crucially, our harness delivers direct physical dispatch energy ($1.252\text{ mJ/op}$ Go vs. $0.196\text{ mJ/op}$ Node Minimal in W2), establishes a formal **Negative Result for Darwin XNU `ri_billed_energy`** ($r = -0.0510, p = 0.580$, centered $\text{OLS } R^2 = -0.676$ vs. the mean, corroborating Fahad et al., 2019 on uncalibrated on-chip energy counters), validates physical CPU energy regressors (`t_exec_ms` $R^2=0.949$, `proc_instructions` $R^2=0.955$, `proc_cycles` $R^2=0.945$), isolates direct SMC `CPU Power` rail orchestrator energy share ($\Phi_{\text{energy, orch}} \in [0.01\%, 1.42\%]$ across W2/W3 in Regime B), and provides an **Analytical & Empirical Memory Bandwidth Estimation ($\text{Model Weights} \div \text{ITL}$)** yielding a global median of $97.0\text{ GB/s}$ ($80.8\%$ of the nominal $120\text{ GB/s}$ LPDDR5X bus) and a mean of $95.9\text{ GB/s}$ ($79.9\%$ in Regime A) to $106.7\text{ GB/s}$ ($88.9\%$ in Regime B; global mean $104.0\text{ GB/s}$ / $86.7\%$), respecting the practical Roofline UMA ceiling ($\approx 86\text{--}88\%$, Imamura, 2026; Benazir & Lin, 2025). Privileges are strictly isolated: agent runtimes and neural daemons execute exclusively in unprivileged user space.
3. **Rigorous Non-Parametric Statistics and Factor Analysis:** Automated computation of Energy-Delay Product ($\text{EDP} = E \times T$), Medians and Interquartile Ranges ($\text{IQR}$), two-sided Mann-Whitney $U$ tests adjusted via step-down Holm-Bonferroni, Cliff's Delta ($d$), Vargha-Delaney effect sizes ($\hat{A}_{12}$ with 95% bootstrap CIs), pairwise Spearman correlation matrices (with Holm-Bonferroni adjusted $p$-values and within-workload stratification), and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) variance decomposition.
4. **Controlled Ablation Matrix, Unified Warm-Cache Session, and Inter-Session Replication ($n=30$):** Physical evaluation across Regime A (cold prefill), Regime B (unified warm KV-cache session), and an independent Inter-Session Replication ($n=30$ per block with native Bun v1.4.4) reveals dual operational regimes: under warm KV-cache amortization (Regime B, prefix hit rate $\ge 97.6\%$), orchestrator execution latency represents only $\Phi_{\text{orch}} \in [0.37\%, 4.65\%]$ of total task duration, with Go, Python, Node.js V8, and Bun achieving intra-session parity within $-1.5\%$ to $+5.8\%$ of the neural baseline (smaller than the $\sim 3\%\text{--}10\%$ cross-session thermal/clock drift in W1, W2, and W4, while W3 vector RAG consistently favors SIMD/BLAS over scalar Go loops by $5.6\%\text{--}5.8\%$). Conversely, compiled Go (PicoClaw) achieves **$3.81\times$ faster mean cold-start invocation** ($9.74\text{ ms}$ vs. $37.15\text{ ms}$ for Node.js V8; median $3.46\times$, $9.67\text{ ms}$ vs. $33.48\text{ ms}$; and $7.29\times$ faster isomorphic worker `--version` startup at $3.04\text{ ms}$ vs. $22.15\text{ ms}$), $2.82\times$ lower physical memory footprint on global average, $3.84\times$ fewer retired instructions, and in 10-turn autonomous ReAct loops (W4) avoiding 54-tool catalog prompt bloat delivers a **$92.53\%$ lower Energy-Delay Product** ($d = -1.0$, $\hat{A}_{12} = 0.0$, $p < 10^{-5}$).

### Multi-OS Reviewer Reproducibility
Recognizing that journal reviewers operate across diverse environments (including Linux x86_64 machines without Apple Metal GPUs or superuser access), our framework incorporates a **reviewer-friendly portable dry-run pipeline (`make dry-run`)**. This command dispatches a self-contained loopback mock server, executes the complete factorial matrix, runs non-parametric hypothesis tests, and renders high-resolution publication figures in under **5 seconds**, backed by continuous integration testing on both `ubuntu-latest` and `macos-latest` GitHub Actions runners.

This manuscript has not been published elsewhere and is not currently under consideration by any other journal. All authors have approved the submission.
